Unit 1: Data Models and Projections

This unit lays the foundation for everything else in the book. It starts with the big picture: what a geographic information system is, the science behind it, and the questions it can answer. It then turns to how a GIS stores the world as data, and how coordinate systems and map projections put that data in the right place on a round Earth. By the end of the unit you can open, explore, edit, query, and join spatial data in QGIS and ArcGIS Pro.

Chapter 1: What Is GIS?

GIS helps people decide where: where a fire is spreading, where rescuers should search, and how to get help there. This chapter looks at GIS in recent events, the four questions that define a geographic problem, and the kinds of questions a GIS can answer. It defines a geographic information system and its five components, separates GISystems from GIScience, and traces the history of GIS, who uses it, and the ethical questions it raises. It ends with the tools used in this book, QGIS and ArcGIS Pro, and how to write a GIS question of your own.

Chapter 2: What Is Geographic Data?

How do you store a tree, a road, or a mountain in a computer? This chapter follows the steps that turn the real world into data: entities become features, and features are stored in a data model, a data structure, and finally machine code. It separates discrete objects from continuous data, and spatial data from attributes, then introduces the two main data models, vector and raster, with the vocabulary of each, from nodes and vertices to cell size and resolution. It ends with how data are stored in files such as the shapefile, why metadata matters, and how to choose between vector and raster.

Chapter 3: Location and Coordinate Systems

How do you say exactly where something is on the Earth? This chapter starts with relative and absolute location, then models the Earth’s shape: Eratosthenes’s measurement, the ellipsoid, and the geoid, with the three heights a GPS and a map report. It explains geodetic datums (NAD27, NAD83, and WGS84) and why the same coordinates can land 100 meters apart in two of them. It ends with latitude and longitude: how they are built, how to write and convert them, how precise they need to be, and why great circles and the graticule matter.

Chapter 4: Map Projections

Every flat map distorts the Earth. This chapter shows how a projection flattens latitude and longitude onto a plane, a cylinder, or a cone, and what each kind keeps and gives up: shape, area, distance, or direction. It explains why Greenland looks as big as Africa on a Mercator map, why web maps are for display and not measurement, and how scale error grows away from standard lines. It then covers the systems used most in North Carolina, UTM and State Plane, reads EPSG codes, and gives a practical method for choosing a projection.

Chapter 5: Attribute Data and Queries

Geometry says where; attributes say what. This chapter looks at the attribute table, its records and fields, the data types that store them and the binary limits behind them, and the levels of measurement that decide which questions make sense. It then teaches attribute queries in SQL, from simple comparisons to AND, OR, NOT, brackets, LIKE, BETWEEN, and IN, with a way to check any query using sets and Venn diagrams, and the trap of NULL values.

Chapter 6: Databases and Table Joins

Most GIS data live in databases. This chapter explains what a database management system does, how relational databases link tables with primary and foreign keys, and how codes such as FIPS codes and GEOIDs serve as keys. It then teaches table joins: the destination and source tables, inner, left, right, and full joins, and how cardinality decides what a join can do, with ways to handle one-to-many relationships. It ends with a census join in QGIS, and the problems that most often break a join.

Chapter 7: Spatial Joins

When two layers share no key, location can link them. This chapter explains spatial predicates such as intersects, within, and contains, and the four kinds of spatial join, from simple inside joins to summarized distance joins. Worked examples match North Carolina towns to their nearest airports and septic systems to the geology beneath them. It covers cardinality, why distances need a projected coordinate system, six questions to plan any spatial join, and the tools that do the work in QGIS and ArcGIS Pro.
