Chapter exercise: Exploratory Spatial Data Analysis

Getting to Know North Carolina’s Births

This exercise goes with Chapter 16, Exploratory Spatial Data Analysis. It uses the dplyr, ggplot2, and tmap skills from Chapters 6 to 10. It should take you about one hour. Read this whole section before you start.

TipGet the exercise file

Download esda-exercise.Rmd

Save it in a new folder called esda-exercise inside your geog215 folder, open it in RStudio, and work through it from top to bottom. Everything on this page is also at the top of the file.

Purpose

Skills you’ll practice. By the end of this exercise you will be able to:

  1. Check the basics of a new table: its size, what one row is, the type of each column, and its missing values.
  2. Calculate and interpret the mean, median, and mode of a variable.
  3. Calculate and interpret the range, interquartile range (IQR), and standard deviation.
  4. Show a distribution with a histogram and a boxplot, choose a bin width, and describe its shape.
  5. Find outliers and decide what to do about them.
  6. Turn counts into a rate, map both, and explain why the rate map tells a different story.

Knowledge you’ll gain. You’ll describe how births and sudden infant deaths (SIDS) were spread across North Carolina’s 100 counties in the 1970s, find out which counties are outliers and why, and see where the rate of SIDS was highest.

Why this matters. Before you fit any model or make any claim, you have to know your data: how big it is, what’s missing, what’s typical, and what’s unusual. Skipping that step is how analysts end up reporting the “average” of a skewed variable, or mapping counts that only show where people live. Exploratory analysis is the first step of the final project, and of every analysis you’ll do after this course.

Task

Work through Parts 1 to 7 in order, top to bottom. In each part:

  • Read the short explanation, then do each step marked with a bullet (•).
  • Wherever you see ___, replace it with the missing code before you run the chunk. A chunk with ___ in it won’t run.
  • Before you run each chunk, predict what it will return. Then run it and check.
  • Answer each question (Q1, Q2, …) in a sentence or two of Markdown, where it says Your answer here.

A note on the data: SIDS is the sudden, unexplained death of a baby under one year old. These are real counts of real losses, from 1974 to 1984; the data are a classic in spatial statistics because the rates vary so much from county to county.

What a good answer looks like. Here is a question, a weak answer, and a strong one.

Question: What is a typical number of births per county?

Weak answer: “3300.”

Strong answer: “The median county had about 2,180 births from 1974 to 1978. The mean is higher, 3,300, because a few big counties (Mecklenburg, Cumberland, Guilford) had far more births than the rest, so the distribution is skewed to the right and the median is the better ‘typical’ value.”

The strong answer gives units and the time period, chooses between the mean and the median, and explains why using the shape of the data.
