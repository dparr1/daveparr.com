Course description

This course will introduce students to data science with a focus on spatial (geographic) data, data that are referenced to a location on Earth’s surface. Students will learn concepts and techniques to apply various facets of data science practice, including data collection, management, and integration, descriptive modeling, exploratory spatial data analysis, and communication via visualization and mapping. Real world examples and datasets spanning physical, social, and health sciences will be used throughout the course.

Learning objectives

Learning objectives describe the skills, knowledge, and abilities students gain from this class. They are grouped by the final grade that shows mastery of them.

  • All students who pass the course (a D or better) will be able to:
    • Describe key concepts of the R programming language.
    • Apply specific vocabulary and concepts to explain facets of putting spatial data science in practice, including geospatial data models, and coordinate systems and projections.
  • For a C or better, students will also be able to:
    • Communicate their ideas with fluency and clarity.
    • Design and write a clearly structured program in R using spatial data.
  • For a B or better, students will also be able to:
    • Design, develop, and implement an R program for a scientific spatial research project.
    • Discuss ethical considerations of collecting, using, and sharing spatial data.
  • For an A, students will also be able to:
    • Design, develop, and implement an R program for a scientific spatial research project in a collaborative format that includes data preparation, data analysis, and visualization.
    • Reflect on learning spatial data science, showing critical thinking skills and problem-solving abilities.

IDEAs in Action learning outcomes (FC-QUANT)

  • Summarize, interpret, and present quantitative data in mathematical forms, such as graphs, diagrams, tables, or mathematical text.
  • Develop or compute representations of data using mathematical forms or equations as models, and use statistical methods to assess their validity.
  • Make and evaluate important assumptions in the estimation, modeling, and analysis of data, and recognize the limitations of the results.
  • Apply mathematical concepts, data, procedures, and solutions to make judgments and draw conclusions.
  • Synthesize and present quantitative data to others to explain findings or to provide quantitative evidence in support of a position.

These are the types of questions you should be able to answer after completing the course:

  1. What is the role of mathematics in organizing and interpreting measurements of the world?
  2. How can mathematical models and quantitative analysis be used to summarize or synthesize data into knowledge and predictions?
  3. What methodology can we apply to validate or reject mathematical models or to express our degree of confidence in them?

Class expectations

Slides will usually be posted on Canvas, although they may not always be available before class, and some content may only be available during lecture. Check Canvas regularly.

The readings include both general reading about data and analysis and tutorials with instructions for accomplishing various tasks. The expectation is that you read them and can summarize their main points.

Class sessions consist of lecture, discussion, individual and group exercises, and “free” work time, in proportions that vary from session to session.

Active attendance is expected. Being an active participant in the classroom is a privilege and responsibility that includes contributing to class discussion, being present, and engaging in the critical thinking processes of the class.

Students do best when they are actively engaged to develop mastery in the material, have a personal connection to what they are learning, and are socially connected to learning with their peers. Learn the concepts and practices of spatial data science, but more importantly, think about how to apply them to your own career path and life goals. Along the way, build relationships with your instructors and your classmates.

Like playing the piano, we can talk about spatial data science, but you won’t improve unless you practice, Practice, PRACTICE! Set aside time each week for the assignments. Spatial data science is not only software: it’s a way of thinking about solving spatial problems that is used in many industries, government organizations, non-profits, and educational institutions.

Grading: how this class works

Specifications grading

This course uses an approach to assessing learning called specifications grading. Instead of grading each assignment with points, everything is assessed with the ESRN rubric (below), based on whether you demonstrate the stated learning goals. Assignments are “bundled” into tiers that reflect a hierarchy of learning goals, and final grades are based on which bundles you satisfactorily complete. The grades are not the goal of the course: they indicate which learning goals you have shown that you accomplished.

Since this approach is new to most students, Dr. Parr will take however much time is needed to make sure you understand the expectations and are poised to succeed at your chosen level. His goal is to help every student reach Satisfactory or better on every part of the course they take on, and to be transparent about expectations throughout the semester.

Assignments

Assignments put spatial data science into practice: they are where you develop your skills in R, programming, and working with spatial data. They are posted on Canvas with detailed instructions and must be submitted on Canvas before the deadline. To count toward your grade, an assignment must meet the specifications in its instructions. If it doesn’t, you’ll get feedback and a chance to resubmit. Work turned in late can’t be resubmitted.

Most students need 2 to 4 hours for an assignment, though some need longer and some assignments are longer than others. Scheduling 4 or 5 hours a week should give you enough time. If you finish early, use the extra time to practice. It will make you more proficient later on!

Projects and presentation

Three projects build on the skills from the assignments. For an A, you need to present at least one of your projects in class, and at least two of your projects should be at the Excellent level. See each project’s instructions for more guidance.

Quizzes

There are 9 online quizzes, one for each module, on the reading and lecture material. They are untimed, and you may retake a quiz once per day. To pass a quiz, you need 100% correct.

Exams

There are two exams: a midterm that covers the first third of the course, and a final exam in our scheduled final exam time. Attendance at both is mandatory. Exceptions are allowed only for University Approved Absences or arrangements made with the instructor before the exam. The point total for both exams is cumulative.

Feedback and revising work

Grades and feedback are often confused. Meaningful, timely feedback that helps you improve is far more useful than a grade, and this grading system is designed to separate the two. Feedback focuses on the key ideas and specifics that will most improve your work, tied to each assessment’s learning objectives. Use it to revise your work or improve future work, and email or visit office hours with specific questions.

Learning is a process, and nobody is perfect the first time. If something isn’t yet Satisfactory, you can resubmit it to make it better.

Tokens

Every student starts the course with 1 token that can be exchanged for some leniency or a chance to revise. Your tokens are tracked in the Canvas gradebook. Tokens are not grades: apart from attendance, you can’t trade tokens for a better grade.

Earn tokens by:

  • Being in the course: everyone starts with 1.
  • Turning in an assignment or project early (on or before the Friday before the Tuesday due date).
  • Getting an Excellent mark on an assignment or project.
  • Coming to class and answering questions in Poll Everywhere.
  • At the instructor’s discretion: for example, asking questions, participating, or sharing relevant, cool class-related things.

Use tokens to:

  • Turn in an assignment or project late (the cost goes up the longer you wait).
  • Resubmit an assignment or project (2 tokens per resubmission).
  • Replace an absence from class (2 tokens).
  • At the instructor’s discretion, for example for poor behavior in extreme circumstances.

Late and early work

The deadline for assignments and projects is 11:59 pm Eastern on Canvas. You can use tokens to turn work in late, and the cost rises the longer you wait. Work isn’t accepted after the last day for late work, and resubmissions aren’t accepted after the last day to resubmit (see the deadlines on the Course materials page). There is no grade penalty for late work, only a token cost, so turning work in late is better than not turning it in. You can earn a token by submitting early!

If you turn in work late for any reason (a poor internet connection, the wrong submission site, Canvas issues), it is late. Tokens are there to give you flexibility. If you run out, late work won’t count toward your final grade.

Negative tokens

If you have negative tokens at the end of the semester, the assignments that used the most tokens for late work or resubmission won’t count toward your final grade. They are removed, starting with the one that used the most tokens, until your balance is zero or positive. If you didn’t use tokens for late work or resubmission, negative tokens won’t affect your grade.

Attendance

Attendance is taken in class with Poll Everywhere. You and your classmates learn better when you’re present, both physically and mentally. If you miss class for any reason, you are still responsible for what was covered, including lecture material, discussions, assessments, and assignment requirements. Ask your classmates what you missed.

Top Student Award

Grades are one measure, often an imperfect one, of your work in a class. Learning is about developing knowledge, skills, relationships, and capabilities that will serve you well in the future. These are principles I’ve found useful for thinking about how to learn:

  • Learning is more important than grades. Grades are, at best, an indicator of your work in a course, but learning is a lifelong process of growth and understanding.
  • Process is more important than product. There is no perfection. A true student works to improve the process of the work, not just the outcome.
  • Curiosity is more important than content. Facts matter, but a willingness to ask meaningful, deep, powerful questions matters a little more. Why is the most important letter in the alphabet.
  • Collaboration is more important than individual effort. The more you work to make others stronger, the stronger you become. Reach out to your instructors and your peers!

One student who best embodies these principles of learning, process, curiosity, and collaboration will receive the Top Student award. Grades don’t decide it: it could be any student.

Frequently asked questions

“I’m not really a computer person…”
That’s not really a question, but I hear it a lot from students who are nervous about the class. Chances are you’re not giving yourself enough credit: I’ve found that 99.9% of students can do the work in this class if they stick with it. Ask questions, get help when you need it, and keep trying.

Is it okay to visit office hours?
Yes! For any reason. Stop by to ask about the class, jobs, or life, or just to chat. Or just nerd out.

What if I almost meet the requirements for the next grade bundle, like everything for a B except one assignment?
Then you’ve earned a C, and there’s nothing wrong with that. In some cases the instructor may consider a +/- grade (a C+ here), but don’t count on it. The specifications are there for a reason.

Can I really pick my grade and the work I want to do?
Yes! As long as your work is Satisfactory or Excellent by each assignment’s specifications, you’re in good shape. If you’re motivated for an A, you’ll learn and do more. There’s no reason anyone should fail this class.

Course policies

Behavior

Respect your peers, your instructors (including substitutes and guests), and yourself. Interact respectfully with your classmates and instructors, and when working in groups, respect the time and thoughts of every person in the group.

Respect your own thoughts and skills, too. In programming it is easy to get confused and frustrated, but confusion is often a sign of deep thought as you make sense of the material. Negative and insulting language about your own abilities will not be tolerated. Any time you feel disrespected, or think you might have to act in a way that could seem disrespectful, talk to me about it.

Communication

With college instructors, teaching assistants, and peers, it’s best to start with formal, direct communication, then adjust once you know how the other person likes to communicate. Personally, I like casual and direct communication. Put the most important point first. Be kind and be brief! Please address your instructor as Dr. Parr or Professor Parr.

Extenuating circumstances

Life can be difficult at times, and most college students go through a hard semester. I’m on your side, and I want you to succeed. If something is keeping you from doing your best work, schedule an office hours visit and talk to me.

Artificial intelligence (AI)

Carolina students are expected to follow these AI guidelines:

  1. AI should help you think, not think for you. In this course, don’t use AI unless an assignment’s or project’s guidelines say you may. Never use AI on an exam or quiz.
  2. Engage responsibly with AI. Evaluate AI output for bias, limitations, inaccuracies, false output, and ethical implications. Don’t put personal or confidential data into these tools.
  3. Use of AI must be open and documented. When an assignment allows it, declare, explain, and cite any use of AI in your work using applicable standards (such as APA, MLA, or course guidelines). You are 100% responsible for your final product.
  4. Follow this syllabus’s AI guidelines. If you’re unsure, ask me. This guidance would be referenced if an issue were referred to Student Conduct for alleged academic misconduct.
